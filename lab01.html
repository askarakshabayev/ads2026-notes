02

Why complexity matters at all

12 mindemo: growth calculator

What to say

  • Sorting 10 numbers finishes instantly with a good algorithm and with a terrible one alike. Sorting 10 million takes hours with the terrible one.
  • So we do not measure seconds — those depend on the machine. We measure how fast the cost grows as n grows.
  • Open the demo and drag the slider. Let the room watch n² fall out of the time limit, and 2ⁿ break entirely.
  • The number to remember for labs: a judge does roughly 10⁸–10⁹ operations per second. From the n in the statement you can pick the complexity you are allowed before writing a line of code.

03

Big-O, and memory separately

13 minboard

What to say

  • Big-O is an upper bound on the growth rate in the worst case.
  • Two things we drop: constant factors (2n and 5n are both O(n)) and lower-order terms (n² + n → O(n²), because n² dominates for large n).
  • Name the neighbours: Θ is a tight bound, Ω is a lower bound. Do not go deep, but the students should have heard the words.
  • Memory is counted separately by the same rules: O(1) for counters, O(n) for an array, O(n²) for an adjacency matrix. Remind them the labs allow 256 MB.

The exponential, live

int fib(int n) {
    if (n <= 1) return n;
    return fib(n - 1) + fib(n - 2);
}

Ask the room: how many times is fib(1) called for n = 40? The answer is on the order of 10⁸ — that is the moment to say why O(2ⁿ) is unusable.

Rules of thumb for the labs: n ≤ 20 → brute force; n ≤ 5·10³ → O(n²); n ≤ 10⁶ → O(n log n); n ≤ 10¹⁸ → only O(log n) or a closed formula.
04

GCD: brute force against Euclid

12 mindemo: side by side

What to say

  • Start with the naive solution — walk down from min(a,b) to the first common divisor. It is correct, and for gcd(12,18) it finishes in 12 checks.
  • Now take gcd(1000000007, 1000000009) — a billion checks. Run the demo and let them read the number off the screen.
  • Derive the identity gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b) with the base case gcd(a, 0) = a. Do three or four steps by hand on the board.
  • Stress the count: O(log min(a,b)) iterations. For 10¹⁸ that is under a hundred. The demo shows the two numbers ten orders of magnitude apart.

For the board

gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b)
gcd(a, 0) = a
long long gcd(long long a, long long b) {
    while (b > 0) {
        long long t = a % b;
        a = b;
        b = t;
    }
    return a;
}
Trap in problem A: a, b ≤ 10¹⁸ does not fit into int. It has to be long long.

05

Binary exponentiation

12 mindemo: step table

What to say

  • Naively, aⁿ costs n multiplications. At n = 10¹⁸ and a billion operations per second that is more than thirty years.
  • The observation: if n is even then aⁿ = (a^(n/2))², and if it is odd then aⁿ = a · a^(n-1). Every step halves the exponent.
  • Do 2¹⁰ by hand — 4 multiplications instead of 10. Then show in the demo that 10¹⁸ takes about 60.
  • Tie it to the binary form: the ones in the binary representation of n are exactly the factors that end up in the answer.

For the board

long long result = 1 % m;
a %= m;
while (n > 0) {
    if (n % 2 == 1)
        result = result * a % m;
    a = a * a % m;
    n /= 2;
}
Two traps in problem B. First: initialise with 1 % m, not 1 — otherwise n = 0, m = 1 gives 1 instead of 0. Second: since m ≤ 10⁹, the product of two residues stays below 10¹⁸ and fits into long long; with a larger m you would need __int128.

06

Sieve of Eratosthenes

14 mindemo: animation

What to say

  • The task is to find all primes up to n. Testing each one separately is O(n√n). The sieve does the same job in O(n log log n).
  • Start the animation and narrate it: take the first uncrossed number — it is prime — and cross out all of its multiples.
  • Stop deliberately on why we start crossing at p² and not at 2p: every smaller multiple has already been crossed by a smaller prime. Hit "Step" at p = 5 and point at 10, 15, 20 already being grey.
  • Memory: a bool array of n entries is O(n). For n = 10⁶ that is about a megabyte — comfortably inside the 256 MB limit.

07

Prime factorization

10 mindemo: trial division

What to say

  • Fundamental theorem of arithmetic: the factorization exists and is unique up to order. 60 = 2·2·3·5, 210 = 2·3·5·7.
  • Dividing by everything up to n is O(n). Going up to √n is enough: a number cannot have two distinct prime factors above √n, their product would exceed n.
  • The moment of the lecture. After the loop the leftover may be greater than 1, and that leftover is a prime factor too. Dropping if (n > 1) is the classic mistake — show it in the demo on 999999999989.
  • For n = 10¹² this is about 10⁶ iterations, a fraction of a second.

For the board

for (long long i = 2; i * i <= n; ++i)
    while (n % i == 0) {
        cout << i << " ";
        n /= i;
    }
if (n > 1) cout << n;   // ← the line everyone forgets
Why √n is enough: if n had two prime factors p, q > √n, then p·q > n. So once the loop ends, what remains is either 1 or a single prime.

08

Stack, Queue, Deque

20 mindemo: playground

What to say

  • Stack — LIFO. A stack of plates: you can only add to and take from the top. Every operation is O(1).
  • Queue — FIFO. A cafeteria line: people join at the back and are served from the front.
  • Deque gives you both ends. Use the playground to show that a stack and a queue are just special cases of it.
  • Name the operations and note they are all O(1): push, pop, top/front, empty, size.
  • Warn them explicitly: pop() and top() on an empty container are undefined behaviour, not an exception. Always check empty() first — this is what breaks submissions in the lab.

10

Defence and scoring

5 mindemo: score calculatorfor the students

What to say

  • Give the timeline first: the lab contest opens today, and two weeks later a separate defence contest runs with 2 problems taken from this same list.
  • Say plainly why the defence multiplies instead of adding: an accepted submission you cannot reproduce is worth nothing. This is the anti-plagiarism mechanism, and it should not come as a surprise in week 5.
  • Point out the practical consequence: solving 5 problems honestly beats copying 10. Five solved with a clean defence gives 1.50; ten copied with a failed defence gives 0.
  • Warn them the two defence problems come from the whole list, not from what they happened to solve — so leaving half the lab untouched is a gamble on both counts.
  • Let them play with the calculator for a minute — it answers "what do I get if…" faster than you can.

The rule

LabContest 1 contains 10 problems, A to J, and is worth 3 points. Solve as many as you can on ejudge.

Two weeks after the lab opens a separate defence contest runs. It holds 2 problems taken from this same list of 10 — the same two for everyone — and you solve them there, on the spot, from scratch.

Your result on the defence contest, as a percentage, is multiplied by the percentage of the lab you solved.

Formula

score = L × Rlab × Rdef
SymbolMeaningLabContest 1
Lpoints this lab contest is worth (from the syllabus)3
Rlabshare of the lab you solved — solved ÷ totalS / 10
Rdefyour result on the 2-problem defence contest, as a share0 … 100 %

Rounded to two decimals. The same formula applies to every lab contest — only L and the number of problems change.

The defence multiplies, it does not add. Ten accepted problems with a zero on the defence contest give 3 × 1.00 × 0 = 0. And it cuts the other way too: acing the defence without having solved the lab gives 3 × 0 × 1.00 = 0. Both halves are required.
